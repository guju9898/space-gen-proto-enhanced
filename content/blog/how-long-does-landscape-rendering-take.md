A landscape rendering takes anywhere from about 30 seconds to a few weeks, depending on who makes it. AI tools like Renderspace return a photorealistic concept from a job-site photo in about 30 seconds, while studios and freelancers building a 3D model typically take days, and longer once revisions start.

For a contractor, the real question is not only how long it takes. It is whether the rendering arrives while the client still cares.

A rendering delay doesn't start when the designer opens the file. It starts the moment you tell a homeowner, "I'll send something next week." From there, every unanswered question has room to grow. Will those pavers look too dark? Is the patio going to swallow the lawn? Would a pergola work better than an open seating area? None of that means the client has lost interest. They simply have nothing concrete to react to yet. A quick visual, especially one you can adjust with specific material and layout prompts, keeps those questions inside the conversation.

## How long does a landscape rendering take from a studio?

Traditional rendering studios build a 3D scene, light it, render it and retouch it. ArchiCGI, a rendering studio, [lists 3 to 5 days](https://archicgi.com/3d-rendering-project-time/) for medium projects such as full house exteriors, and 1 to 3 weeks for larger exterior projects. They also note that each revision round adds time.

What drives a studio timeline:

- **The brief.** Missing measurements or references mean back-and-forth before work starts.
- **Modeling.** Without CAD files, the property has to be built from scratch.
- **Revisions.** Every "can we try it with a different paver" goes back into the queue.
- **The queue itself.** Your job waits behind everyone else's.

## How long does a freelancer take?

Freelance marketplaces are often quicker to hire, but timelines vary widely by artist, workload and revisions. The same drivers apply: a 3D model has to be built, and every change is a new request. ## Why does turnaround time matter so much for contractors?

Because interest has a shelf life. The homeowner is most excited on the day you walk the yard together. Every day between that visit and the moment they see something is a day for second thoughts, other bids and "let's wait until spring."

Rapidly generating photorealistic renderings helps contractors win bids faster. When homeowners see their own space finished before the quote, they commit sooner.

I think contractors often ask too much of the first rendering. They want it polished enough to be a final presentation before they even know whether the homeowner likes the direction. That reverses the order of the sale. Start with a rough decision, not a finished design: show the space, ask what feels off, and use a custom prompt to swap brick for limestone or move the pergola. Save the measured drawings and detailed modeling for the stage when precision actually matters.

## When is it worth waiting for a slower rendering?

Speed is not everything. A detailed studio model can make sense for large commercial work, marketing hero images, or when exact dimensions have to be shown in a presentation. A sensible split for many contractors:

- **First visit:** a fast concept from a photo to agree on direction.
- **After the client commits to a direction:** more concepts or a done-for-you pack if you need a set.
- **For build work:** your normal design, engineering and permitting process.
